---
title: "The Frequency That Invented the Stunt Twice"
date: 2026-08-10
---

*104.7 did both halves before Vancouver made it viral, and the archive did exactly nothing to help us remember.*

This started because Tidal made a smart playlist from one Primitive Radio Gods track and, somehow, did not miss once.

The seed was "Standing Outside a Broken Phone Booth with Money in My Hand." What followed was Superdrag, Luscious Jackson, Eels, Nada Surf, Cake, Local H, Cracker, the Verve Pipe, Dishwalla. Not merely the right artists. The right songs.

It had reconstructed an extremely specific patch of late-'90s alternative radio: detached vocals, fuzzy guitars, literate slacker melancholy, novelty-hit proximity without quite becoming novelty music. Songs heard through a Ford's FM radio while passing a Hills. Roughly 1:17 on a weekday afternoon in 1997.

Whatever genre model and sample size it was working from, they were apparently enormous.

The playlist reminded me of a radio station whose callsign I could not remember. My brain returned WFNX, Boston's 101.7---a different station from a later layer of my life.

Wrong station. Wrong city. Wrong decade.

The Pittsburgh station was **WNRQ, 104.7: The Revolution**.

That was a fucking great name.

WNRQ launched on August 29, 1995, at 4 p.m. Its first song was "Send Me on My Way" by Pittsburgh's own Rusted Root. It played modern rock for slightly less than a year before being sold to Secret Communications, which already owned competing alternative station WXDX, "The X."

Owning two stations with overlapping formats was apparently one revolution too many.

On June 1, 1996, The Revolution played its last song: "Killing in the Name" by Rage Against the Machine.

Then it became smooth jazz.

This is almost offensively perfect. The station began with "Send Me on My Way," ended with "Fuck you, I won't do what you tell me," and was immediately replaced by soprano saxophone. A complete history of American dissent in three programming decisions.

You would think this particular use of "Killing in the Name" would be better known. Search for stories about radio stations playing the song during a format change, though, and the version you mostly find happened in Vancouver in 2022.

KiSS Radio 104.9 had just laid off several popular hosts when it began playing "Killing in the Name" continuously. The station kept it going for about 30 hours. The only interruptions were callers requesting something else and being denied, callers requesting the song they were already hearing, and DJs behaving as though the transmitter had been seized by people whose demands were limited to more Rage Against the Machine.

People naturally concluded that disgruntled employees had occupied the station.

The story escaped local radio almost immediately. It reached [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2022/jun/29/canadian-radio-station-rage-against-the-machine-song-nonstop), [Pitchfork](https://pitchfork.com/news/vancouver-radio-station-plays-rage-against-the-machines-killing-in-the-name-on-repeat-for-hours/), CBS, Rolling Stone, and Tom Morello. People tuned in from other countries to hear the apparent insurrection.

It was a format-change stunt. KiSS became alternative station SONiC Radio.

Fine. It was funny. It was well executed. It worked enormously better than anyone expected.

It also worked better than the station did. SONiC lasted three years before abruptly becoming [KiSS Throwbacks](https://broadcastdialogue.com/kiss-throwbacks-format-launches-on-former-metro-vancouver-sonic-radio-stations/) in June 2025. The articles announcing its birth remain more culturally legible than the station itself. The content outlived the institution it advertised.

The Vancouver stunt looked like a particularly internet-era event: layoffs happen, somebody barricades himself in the booth, social media notices, Rage plays until management retakes the building.

Except Pittsburgh had already done almost exactly that.

Not WNRQ. Same frequency, earlier callsign.

On May 16, 1989, 104.7 was WYDD and preparing to become WNRJ, "Energy 105." To announce the change, the station made a tape loop from Information Society's "What's on Your Mind (Pure Energy)."

Actually, "the song" oversells the amount of musical variety involved. The loop eventually focused on Leonard Nimoy saying **"pure energy."**

It ran from Tuesday evening until 7:30 Wednesday evening: about 25½ hours.

Listeners decided something had gone wrong inside the station. They called WYDD. Then they called the police. Then 911. Then the FBI. Police reportedly stopped at the station four times to make sure the DJs had not been murdered, kidnapped, or perhaps driven insane by Leonard Nimoy.

The station eventually added announcements explaining that the stuck record was intentional. This did not prevent people from listening for hours to determine whether something was wrong.

General manager Bob Hank's explanation afterward, reported by [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1989/05/19/Pure-energy-radio-stunt-draws-too-much-attention/6884611553600/), was perfect:

> "We were just trying to draw a little bit of attention. We never dreamed it would go this far."

That may be the most Pittsburgh sentence ever spoken by a radio executive. Accidentally cause a minor media incident, explain that you weren't trying to make a whole thing out of it, then get back to work.

So the same Pittsburgh frequency supplied both halves of Vancouver's famous 2022 stunt.

In 1989, 104.7 looped one piece of audio for 25½ hours while listeners concluded something had happened to the staff and emergency services checked the building.

In 1996, 104.7 used "Killing in the Name" as the final word of a dead format.

In 2022, a station 2,500 miles away combined the loop with the Rage song, ran it for a nearly identical length of time, caused the same speculation about rebellion inside the booth, and received international coverage for it.

That is a much better story than the one I thought I was writing. It is also a more annoying one.

My first instinct was that Pittsburgh had done the interesting thing and neglected to archive it. This would be a familiar story. Local radio was disposable. A DJ made a joke, a station changed formats, a club hosted its final show, and unless somebody had a cassette running, the event disappeared into the air immediately after it happened.

Except that isn't what happened here.

The receipts exist.

The 1989 stunt was reported by UPI and the *Los Angeles Times*. The station changes were recorded in radio-industry publications whose old issues are now digitized, public, and free. The chronology is sitting in the [history of 104.7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WPGB). Dates, callsigns, songs, ownership changes: all preserved well enough that a stranger can reconstruct the entire sequence decades later.

Pittsburgh did archive it.

The archive simply did not become memory.

Those are different systems, and we have spent decades improving the first while pretending it automatically feeds the second. Storage is not retrieval. A receipt can remain perfectly intact forever without ever acquiring enough standing to affect the story people tell.

The receipts existed. Their existence changed nothing.

Which brings me back to the smart playlist.

The algorithm reconstructed what that lost period of radio **felt like** from a single Primitive Radio Gods song. It recovered the texture with unnerving accuracy: the small emotional neighborhood connecting Superdrag, Eels, Nada Surf, Cake, Local H, and everything else that sounded correct through cheap car speakers in 1997.

But it did not recover the event.

The institutional archive preserved what happened but not what it felt like. The statistical system recovered what it felt like but not what happened. Neither one joined the two.

I did, mostly by getting the callsign wrong.
